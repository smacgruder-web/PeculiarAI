------------------------------------------------------------
DAY 1 — WHY I'M WRITING THIS
------------------------------------------------------------
They told me to "manage" it. I built something instead.
I'm a veteran and the founder of an AI company. PTSD doesn't clock out
when the deploy is over — it just changes uniforms. So I started using the
tools I build to keep my own head in the fight. Daily, I'll show you what
actually helps. Not therapy. Not a cure. Just one vet's operating system.
What's the one thing you wish tech understood about your bad days?

------------------------------------------------------------
DAY 2 — THE 3AM LOOP
------------------------------------------------------------
The worst part isn't the flashback. It's the loop after, at 3am, when your
brain won't stand down.
I taught a local AI on my laptop to be a "stand-down protocol": I dump the
spiral into it, it reflects it back without judgment and walks me to a
breathing drill. No cloud. No one reading it. Just a mirror that doesn't
flinch. If you've been there, you know the loop eats the whole night.
What would you tell your 3am self if you could?

------------------------------------------------------------
DAY 3 — JOURNALING WITHOUT THE BLANK PAGE
------------------------------------------------------------
PTSD makes the blank page hostile. So I stopped starting from zero.
Every morning my AI asks me three fixed questions — sleep, triggers, one
good thing — and logs it locally. Six months in, the pattern is obvious:
my worst days follow three bad nights, every time. The machine saw it
before I did. Pattern recognition is a soldier's skill. AI just holds the
ledger. Do you track anything, or just white-knuckle it?

------------------------------------------------------------
DAY 4 — GROUNDING THAT TRAVELS
------------------------------------------------------------
Grounding exercises work. Remembering to do them mid-episode doesn't.
I put a one-tap "ground" command on my phone that pushes a 5-4-3-2-1
script with haptics. No app store. No account. Just a button for when the
world goes loud. The point isn't the AI — it's removing the friction
between the tool and the moment you need it. What's your go-to when it
spikes?

------------------------------------------------------------
DAY 5 — DEBRIEFING MYSELF
------------------------------------------------------------
In the field we did after-action reviews. At home, nobody debriefs you.
So I do. I talk to my local model like a battle buddy and run an AAR:
what happened, what triggered it, what I'd do different. It's structured,
it's private, and it ends with a plan — not a spiral. After-action culture
isn't just for ops. It's for the quiet wars too. Who debriefs you?

------------------------------------------------------------
DAY 6 — THE TOOL THAT DIDN'T MAKE ME FEEL WEAK
------------------------------------------------------------
I did not need a smarter lecture. I needed a place to talk that did not
put an audience on me. Local stand-down: I dump the loop, it reflects it,
then it walks me to one next action. No cloud. No scoreboard. If the night
is bigger than a tool, I call a person — 988, press 1. Hard line stays:
the model reflects; it does not diagnose or prescribe. Where's your line?

People ask why a PTSD guy builds AI instead of "resting."
Because discipline is the only thing that's ever worked for me, and AI is
